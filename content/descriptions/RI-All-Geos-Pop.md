## How to Lie with Maps
I recently started reading *How to Lie With Maps* by Mark Monmonier to learn some fundamental ideas behind map making. This book has been recommended to me many times and is cited as a sort of bible for novice cartographers. This book is a great exploration into all the choices mapmakers have to make and how those choices can change the story the map tells. It has got me thinking about the biases we put into maps and how much control a mapmaker can have on the end result. I decided to create an interactive map to explore this idea.

This map depicts the population in Rhode Island from the American Community Survey 5-Year Estimate. In the top right is a control to switch between various geographic boundaries. Rhode Island counties are relatively large compared to the state and cover wide east-west stretches of land. When looking at the county level it could be reasonable to conclude that north-western Rhode Island has a lot of people living there since it lies in the highest population county in the state, Providence County. When we zoom in to smaller geographies like census tract, it becomes clear that Providence County's population is concentrated around Providence and not in the western part of the county. Depending on how we pick the geographic boundaries, the same underlying data can tell very different stories.
